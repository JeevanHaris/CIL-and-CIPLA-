By 2030, AI Could Make GDP Zoom, And Jobs Dry Up 
Here are three scenarios drawn up by economists at the Anthropic Institute, all based on the US economy. The base 
scenario is a world without AI. But at every level of AI adoption, the economy grows, and job losses rise:
Modest: Where AI 
spreads slowly and has 
relatively small effects
Substantial: Where it reaches

mostly automates affected work
Extreme: Where AI performs almost half the work 
now done in management, professional, sales and office 
jobs, and 90% of affected work is automated
Note that these are scenarios, not predictions. The one most likely to materialise “may become clearer within a year or two”, the report said

In Anthropic’s scenarios, as AI takes on more knowledge work, white-collar employment falls while jobs elsewhere grow.

may find it difficult to become an electrician — that may give rise to sustained increases in unemployment,” the report said

Gurgaon: Chintels Paradiso, where a vertical
collapse across five floors killed two residents
in Feb 2022, is set to be razed and completely re-
built, with Supreme Court clearing the decks
for a time-bound redevelopment plan for the
Sector 109 condominium.
A bench of  justices Pamidighantam Sri Nara-
simha and Alok Aradhe ordered that the redeve-
lopment should start by March 31, 2027. Sobha will
undertake the project along with Chintels India
Pvt Ltd (CIPL), subject to statutory approvals. The
court fixed Jan 1, 2027, as deadline for residents
still occupying some of  the unsafe towers to sign
agreements, vacate their flats, and hand them
over . The judges noted that around 80-90 people
were still living in flats declared unsafe, and clari-
fied that no extension should be entertained. The
physical handover of  the rebuilt apartments will
have to be completed within the 24-month clock .
The Paradiso project had 532 flats across ni-
ne towers spread over 12 acres. Towers D, E, F , G
and H, forming Phase I of  the project, have al-
ready been demolished. Towers A, B, C and J,
part of  Phase II, will be razed and are part of
the redevelopment plan. The redeveloped pro-
ject will have six towers with 800 flats.
In Feb 2022, a portion of  Flat 603 in Tower D
collapsed, triggering an implosion in the floors
below , and killing two residents. An FIR was re-
gistered at Bajghera police station and later refer-
red to CBI by Haryana govt. Structural audits, in-
cluding those conducted by IIT-Delhi, followed.
Under the settlement approved by the apex
court, owners choosing redevelopment of  the-
ir flats will have to pay Rs 1,000 per sqft, with no
additional or hidden financial burden. The
new flats will have the same carpet area as the
existing ones, subject to a maximum 3% varia-
tion, and must match the specifications, mate-
rials, fixtures, fittings, finishes and amenities
being offered by Sobha to new buyers.
The rebuilt homes for residents of  towers
A, B, C and J will come up on the existing Phase
II land. CIPL has also been directed to pay a fix-
ed rent to eligible homeowners from Jan 31,
2027, until physical possession of  their new
flats is given. It must initially deposit Rs 5 crore
in an escrow account, which will be reviewed
every six months and replenished if  required.
Residents of  the four towers will also get Rs
40,000 each as one-time relocation charges.
Of  the 532 homeowners in Paradiso, 196 had
opted for buyback and completed the required
documents, 164 had signed redevelopment ag-
reements, while 172 are yet to exercise their op-
tion or finalise arrangements.

New Delhi: A nationwide study to assess the
impact of  air pollution and other types of  en-
vironmental exposure on human health
across 20 cities remains incomplete nearly se-
ven years after it was started. The Union envi-
ronment ministry’s working group noted in
May that the studies were “highly delayed”. It
pushed for the urgent need to complete them.
The development was highlighted through
a compliance affidavit filed by the ministry be-
fore NGT in proceedings stemming from a TOI
report published in Oct 2023 titled “Feeling an-
xious? Toxic air could be to blame”.
NGT had directed the ministry to clarify
whether the apex committee on environmen-
tal health and its working group were still
functioning or had been reconstituted, and to
place their minutes on record.
The affidavit states that the working group
met on May 8 this year to review the progress
of  the National Environmental Health Profile
(NEHP) studies, which were started in 2019-20
in 20 cities, which are Delhi, Ludhiana, Kan-
pur , Raipur , Guwahati, Patna, Dhanbad, Bhu-
baneswar , Kolkata, Shillong, Bengaluru, Hy-
derabad, Visakhapatnam, Chennai, Thiruva-
nanthapuram, Ahmedabad, Mumbai, Jaipur ,
Bhopal and Panaji.
At the meeting, chaired by the ministry jo-
int secretary , the group noted the need to comp-
lete the studies as they were “highly delayed”.
The project has a sanctioned cost of  Rs
12.79 crore, with about Rs 8 crore released to
implementing institutions through Central
Pollution Control Board, the nodal agency .
The latest review revealed continued delays. It
also showed that 16 institutions had sought ex-
tensions after responding to the ministry’s
March request for details of  progress, expen-
diture and timelines.

voidable circumstances and simultaneous
commitments”. It suggested involving anot-

ports for the compilation of  the final report.

extension. Cities where project activities and
data collection are still under way have been
given three months to complete them, follo-
wed by another three months for AIIMS to
compile and analyse the data, after which the
minutes state that the “project tenure may be
extended for six months”.
The delay was highlighted when the apex
committee on environmental health met on
March 21, 2023. It was then told that the project
had been extended up to March 31, 2024.

Pune: At least 218 districts
across the country are sho-
wing drought-like conditions
based on rainfall received
over the last four weeks, with
IMD’s Standardised Precipi-
tation Index (SPI) placing
them in the moderate, severe
or extreme dry categories.
SPI is a rainfall-based indi-
cator that compares precipita-
tion received over a given pe-
riod with the historical rain-
fall expected for that period.
The latest four-week pe-
riod of  Aug 13-Sept 9, 2026, has
30 more such districts than the
corresponding period of  Aug
17-Sept 13, 2023, which recor-
ded 188 districts in these cate-
gories. The year 2023 witnes-
sed a below-normal monsoon,
too, and was the last strong
El Niño year before 2026.
Although the overall num-
ber of  dry-category (inclu-
ding mild, moderate, severe or
extreme dry) districts has de-
clined marginally from 469 in
2023 to 454 in the same four-we-
ek period, the severity of  dry-
ness recorded has increased.
The shift comes alongside a
sharp fall in mildly dry dis-
tricts, from 281 to 236, sugges-
ting that more districts with
below-normal rainfall are
experiencing relatively more
pronounced rainfall stress.
As per an IMD map, a mar-
ked presence of  drier districts
can be seen in the Marathwa-
da and Vidarbha regions of
Maharashtra, as well as many
districts of  Gujarat, Andhra
Pradesh, Rajasthan and Meg-
halaya, and some parts of  Pun-
jab-Haryana. The number of
districts in moderate, severe
and extreme wet categories
has risen from 58 in 2023 to 86
this year , pointing to sharper
rainfall extremes at both ends.
Satyaban Bishoyi Ratna,
head of  climate application
and user interface, IMD Pune,
told TOI, “Districts falling in
the moderately dry , severely
dry and extremely dry catego-
ries can be considered to be ex-
periencing drought-like con-
ditions in terms of  recent rain-
fall.” He clarified the SPI-ba-
sed assessment relates to the
rainfall situation and does not
incorporate factors such as
temperature or soil moisture.
“Dry conditions are being
witnessed in many parts of  the
country over the recent four
weeks. If  such conditions per-
sist, they could raise further
concerns for agriculture and
water resources,” Ratna said.
“The impact is more direct in
rural areas, where rainfall is
closely linked to agriculture
and other aspects of  daily life.”
The number of  moderately
dry districts has risen from 97
in 2023 to 120 this year , while se-
verely dry districts increased
from 50 to 65. Extremely dry
districts, however , declined
from 41 to 33. The key changeis
in the severity of  recent rain-
fall deficiency: there are fewer
districts in the mildly dry cate-
gory , but more in the moderate
and severe categories. The da-
ta has also shown an increase
in districts experiencing more
pronounced wet conditions.
Severely wet districts have in-
creased from 1 1 to 26 and extre-
mely wet districts from 14 to 16.
Ratna added the latest 
data captures only recent ra-
infall conditions and does
not reflect the cumulative 
effect of  rainfall deficits 
earlier in the 2026 monsoon.

India is hosting chapter 18 of the BRICS 
Summit now 
— how do you analyse the 
significance of BRICS today?
  I think of  BRICS as being represent-
ative of  the Global South, a geopolitical 
unit which is a real counterpoint to the 
G7. BRICS was actually 
envisioned that way 
and today, BRICS  
nations count for over 
half  of  humanity and 
the largest emerging 
markets. These are not 
third-world economies 
today but places where 
tremendous growth is taking place and 
most young people are to be found.
However , for me, BRICS is an existen-
tial collaboration because it is the way for 
us as a planet, particularly less developed 
regions, to grow in a sustainable manner . 
If  that doesn’t happen, all bets are off  for 
Earth. This effort must be undertaken in 
a relatively short time because we are  
already seeing potentially catastrophic 
impacts of  climate change — and the peo-
ple most affected are in the Global South.
Climate change is an existential ques-
tion for BRICS countries now and the  
Summit is a huge opportunity for serious  
collaboration, not just geopolitical symbol-
ism. BRICS can achieve practical South-
to-South problem-solving around sustain-
ability , food security , climate resilience, 
affordable, inclusive innovation and  
employing young people in productive 
ways. BRICS is a historic opportunity and 
it’s wonderful it’s hosted by India because 
India is truly at the epicentre of  all these 
complex dynamics.
Many BRICS economies had started down 
the route to sustainability 
— how has the 
Trumpian era, with its heated pursuit of 
fossil fuels, impacted their commitments?
  I actually think the Trumpian impacts 
have, if  anything, made it clearer to the 
BRICS countries how important energy 
independence and security are — and 
relatedly , how much more important  
renewable energy becomes since it’s 
clearly precarious to rely only on fossil 
fuels. For India, for instance, which was 
always so dependent on its energy  
coming from the oil-rich countries of  the 
Gulf, it’s now clear our transition to  
renewables is a pressing need, both for 
sustainability and energy security .  
      China, which had a lot of  oil 
reserves, perhaps anticipated 
disruptions around the use 
of  oil as a way for the United 
States to get purchase on it 
strategically — hence, it 
moved towards renewables, 
undertaking electrification 
of  its mobility system. Today , 
China’s auto industry and bat -
tery sectors are world leaders. 
They have a surfeit of  such 
technologies and can export 
these, which means others can 
ride on their extraordinary innovation 
and capacity in renewables, solar panels, 
battery storage, EVs, etc.
Other BRICS economies are similarly 
visionary — consider Brazil, an astonish-
ing country and in some ways, a bit like 
India, very large, democratic and diverse. 
They understood the importance of  energy 
security and sustainability a while ago and 
they are now global pioneers in biofuels. 
They have large amounts of  forest cover 
and agricultural 
products. A lot of  
that bio-waste is 
used for making 
biofuels. Russia has plenty of  fossil fuels 
and it doesn’t look like it may transition to  
renewables anytime soon but China, India, 
Brazil and South Africa — rising eco-  
nomies — are moving down that path.
The Trumpian era is making 
it clear to these nations that 
they have to be self-sufficient 
in energy — and renewables 
help us do this. I’d add that 
in the renewables mix, we 
should think about nuclear 
power too where India has 
made strides with small 
modular reactors, etc.
India’s adopting more clean 
power
 — but with the Iran 
crisis, there’s a new push for 
coal. How do you see India’s 
dilemma, painfully aware of climate 
change but also having to deliver on the 
energy demands of its citizens?
  This is palpably true. Our energy needs 
are enormous and growing and they are 
not likely to be satisfied purely by  
renewables, even with storage increas-
ing. Realistically , coal will be an impor-
tant source of  power for some time and 
fossil fuels will remain prominent — this 
is why , beyond stating ‘2070’, India has 
not made dramat-
ic commitments 
as to when it’ll 
achieve Net Zero.
India must meet its development 
needs and the challenge of  raising a very 
large number of  people out of  poverty or 
lower-middle-income status. So, there is 
a continuing fossil fuel dependence 
alongside other issues like transi-
tioning the jobs of  people work-
ing in mining, etc.
That said, India is beating 
all kinds of  predictions and 
expectations on how quickly 
that transition could happen. 
There have been remarkable 
developments in the price of  
renewable energy and thereby , 
its application. I recently saw an 
interesting depiction of  three 
sources of  energy use — fossil 
fuels, renewables and biofuels. 
Y ou could track the trajectories over time 
of  different countries as to where they got 
their energy from as they grew econo-  
mically — until lately , the West was very 
high on the fossil fuel side. Only recently 
did their path move more towards renewa-
bles. Remarkably , India moved onto the 
trajectory towards clean energy sooner for 
where it is at that level of  growth. We are 
turning much more rapidly than many 
expected in the direction of  renewables. 
With electrification, we are benefiting from 
other BRICS nations, such as China, while 
India’s made impressive strides in the  
mobility sector , particularly scaling manu-
facture of  two-wheelers, including batter-
ies and EVs, and figuring out frugal ways 
to charge these. Currently , India is not more 
sustainable than China or the West and we 
are still dependent on 
coal, but our trajectory is 
an interesting one — if  
we throw nuclear into the 
mix, this could become 
even more impressive.
After the recent Nepal floods, calls for 
climate justice are growing 
— what 
position could BRICS, both transitioning 
to sustainability but also emitting, take on 
climate change, which, as Nepal has 
argued, no longer comes only from the 
West but also from India and China?
  Here is where BRICS can do something 
truly meaningful which isn’t just using 
power rhetoric against the West. In terms 
of  South-South problem-solving and work-
ing for environmental justice, there could 
be a BRICS Climate and Resilience Fund 
now , a real platform with significant fund-
ing where these economies all contribute, 
like the World Bank or UN model but 
BRICS-specific. These economies could 
identify ecological problems they all share 
and collaborate and cooperate on how to 
grow sustainably and adapt to climate 
change, even as it’s happening and  
affecting your citizens who are 
among the most vulnerable 
populations on the planet.
Recognising this and 
making it a priority , BRICS 
could create a fund to back 
resilience-related initia -
tives around renewable  
energy , grid upgrades, sto-
rage, climate adaptation,  
water and agriculture. These 
are opportunities all these 
countries share and a new 
platform could be built which 
is strategic, focused on common problems 
and where money and ideas are pooled.
In this, frugal innovation is a strong 
route for change. The BRICS economies 
all share certain limitations. They could 
thus distribute approaches to innovation 
which responds in creative ways to  
resource constraints. That has resonance 
already — when I and my co-authors wrote 
our books on frugal innovation, we found 
they were almost immediately translated 
into Portuguese for Brazil, Mandarin for 
China, Russian for Russia and French for 
Francophone countries in Africa. The 
BRICS Sumit could thus grow a frugal  
innovation network that links universities, 
startups, development banks, research 
labs, space centres and other platforms 
that bring these nations 
closer  together by  
producing practical  
solutions.
An entire spectrum of  
collaborative possibility 
is opening up now —  
consider the space sector where it is  
becoming more affordable to make and 
send rockets. This facilitates getting data, 
such as detailed readings on farms and 
giving farmers very precise advice then 
on how to use water and fertiliser best, 
based on satellite imagery . AI could also 
be used responsibly by BRICS economies 
to help deal with climate change and  
adaptation frugally . With BRICS, we could 
see the meeting of  all these developing 
technologies, from AI to quantum comput-
ing and space research, in ways that focus 
on real problems.
A BRICS platform won’t need a Silicon 
Valley-type approach where people are 
spending more and more on making  
bigger data centres — it could focus on the 
actual needs of  people in the Global South 
and use these resources in affordable,  
accessible and equitable ways.

or the past several years, UK consumer group “Which?” has
reported that Black Friday deals are often bogus. When a store
claims the price of  a product has been slashed from £500 to
£400, chances are it didn’t cost £500 in the first place. Last year , none
of  the deals it checked turned out to be the lowest price of  the past 
12 months. You might have experienced the same kind of  fudging
in festive sales – both online and in-store – here. Something you’ve
been waiting to buy in a Diwali sale, becomes costlier when the 
sale starts, and the discounted price turns out to be the same as or
more than the pre-sale price.
Good news is this won’t be allowed 
on e-commerce platforms in India from 
Jan 1. Under new rules, the announcement
of  a discount will also have to reveal the
product’s “prior price”. This is not an 
arbitrary value but the lowest price at which the product was sold
on the same platform in a 30-day period before the discount 
announcement. It gives buyers a way to ascertain the genuineness
of  discounts. Otherwise, when expectations from sales are running
high, it is easy to be deceived.
Other major markets already have such safeguards. US Federal
Trade Commission requires that a “former price” in a n ad be a 
“bona fide price”. In California, a former price must  have been 
the “prevailing market price” within 90 days precedi ng the ad.
EU introduced a prior price rule in 2022, with the s ame 30-day 
window , but applied it to in-store sales as well. An d that makes
sense because price trackers can give you price histories of  products
sold online, but you have no way of  knowing whether that shirt
marked “60% off ” in a store is a bargain or not. India should extend
the rule offline too.

The 18th BRICS Summit
is a grand display of
New Delhi’s diplomatic
outreach. This is India’s
fourth time as host, but
the first in an expanded
BRICS format.
The idea of  a BRIC
group may have been
conceived by Goldman Sachs in 2001, 
but BRIC as a Leaders forum was
born only in 2009. The timing 
speaks for itself. The Leaders G20
was created a year earlier , in 
response to 2008 financial crisis, 
in which all BRIC nations were
included. This should have been
adequate representation for them in
global economic governance. As it
transpires, this was not deemed to be
so, and they decided to upgrade their
own group to Leaders level.
For India to sit in a group, which
played up its non-West character , was
much more challenging in 2009 than
it is today . India’s relations with US
were then at a historic high, following
the nuclear deal in 2008. India’s 
decision to go to Yekaterinburg was
therefore bold and, not surprisingly ,
looked askance by Washington elites.
Yet, India went along because of
other equations that were germane
to the decision.
The US-led West, on whom 
India had just the other day – in the
biggest leap of  faith since the days of
non-alignment – placed its bets, was 
engulfed in an existential crisis. The 
crisis was forced, and triggered a sense
of  helplessness and resentment. India,
like the rest of  the world, was paying 
the price for American profligacy . Trust
in the infallibility of  the market, and 
integrity of  Bretton Woods Institutions,
had suddenly been lifted.
For the rest, the West’s relations 
with Russia were still cordial, despite
incipient signs of  trouble, and of  course,
China was the poster child of  globalisation.
It had, by then, lent the scale that 
underwrote western economic prosperity.
But that was not all. The 2008 crisis 
made the West turn to China to bail 
out the global economy . China has not
looked back since then.
That is the story of  the origin of  a
process that has continued uninterrupted
since 2009.
If  China has emerged as the sole
challenger to American economic
supremacy , it is not to blame. If  Russia
was the source of  cheap and abundant
energy that first fuelled Europe’s 
industrial might, and later China’s, it is
not to blame. Question to ask is, where
was India in all this?
The tables have turned today . It is
not the collapse of  Soviet Union that
is the end of  history . But it’s the end 
of  globalisation. Global economy is 
fragmented, weaponised and volatile.
The international trade architecture
is in a state of  rigor mortis . India’s
relations with White House are a shadow
of  what they once were. US is spending
its way out of  economic trouble, with 
a record $40tn debt, unconcerned by 
its global consequences.
Expansion of  BRICS in 2024 was,
thus, more the expression of  frustration
by middle powers against the double
whammy of  Covid and Ukraine war ,
than the fruit of  brilliant Chinese 
diplomacy . China only sensed the mood
and seized the moment.
Between them, Russia, Saudi Arabia,
UAE and potentially Iran, and China
hold the keys to India’s current and 
future energy security and critical raw
material and mineral needs, and thereby
its economic trajectory .
The proposition that India does not
need BRICS and is sufficiently insured
by its good relations with the West is a 
fallacy . India needs all sides, and needs 
to take a leaf  out of  the Chinese 
playbook by maximising advantage 
from all relationships.
As BRICS leaders meet, major power
relations are headed into uncharted 
territory . US-Russia relations could 
well be on the cusp of  big developments.
US-China relations are headed towards
carefully managed competition, if  not 
a division of  labour . The euphoria of  
decoupling has given way to the realism
of  derisking and cohabitation. In this
moment of  uncertainty , India’s presence
in BRICS is even more essential than 
it was in 2009. India is systemically 
more important today than it was
in 1991, 2008 or 2014.
Guiding the future trajectory 
of  BRICS will be a key task for 
India. That China is the dominant
power in BRICS is not news 
anymore. That ship sailed in 2008.
What India must do is to ensure that
the BRICS agenda is not hijacked
by it, or used as a stepping stone

A repeat of  India’s success in forging

Summit in 2023, is certainly possible
in the case of  Iran.
The totality of  Indian foreign policy
obviously includes trade, tech, defence
and investment relations with US and 
the West. It also means simultaneous
engagement with bilateral, plurilateral
and multilateral platforms.

and isolate our own essence from what
we so ardently like. Where do we end

Which explains role play in bedrooms.

mooching off  me.”

Mumbai: The $136.4 billion

bonds, turning a successful
foreign-currency fund-rai-
sing exercise into a domestic
liquidity-management hea-
dache.RBI will sell Rs 1 lakh
crore of  govt bonds maturing
in FY29-32 in three tranches
on Sept 17, 21 and 28 to drain
surplus liquidity . This is its
first net bond sale in two ye-
ars. Bond yields rose after the
announcement, with the 10-
year yield up six basis points
to 7.04% and the five-year yi-
eld nearly 10 basis points to
6.62%.
Banks raised $127 billion
through the special forex mo-
bilisation scheme, creating
surplus rupee liquidity and
pushing overnight rates be-
low the repo rate. Markets
had expected measures inclu-
ding FX swaps, MSS bonds,
OMO sales and a possible
CRR hike.
Exploring oprions: RBI
In an interview with a TV
channel, RBI governor San-
jay Malhotra said the central
bank had several options to
manage liquidity beyond
VRRR, including open mar-
ket operations and FX swaps.
He said “nothing is off  the
table.” RBI has also been
using dollar-rupee swaps to
reduce banks’ rupee hol-
dings. FCNR(B) deposits mo-
bilised through the recently
concluded RBI swap facility
totalled $127.2 billion. Mal-
hotra said the flows were “ve-
ry robust” and reflected
strong investor confidence in

damentals. He said the exerci-
se showed that foreign capital
could be mobilised within a
short period and strengthe-
ned financial stability and ex-
ternal sector resilience.

had helped stabilise the forex
market. “It has given us the li-
quidity at the same time, and
it has improved sentiments,”
he said. Nearly half  of  the de-
posits, or around 48.5-50%,
have a five-year tenure. About
42% fall in the three-to-four-
year maturity bracket and
around 9% in the four-to-five-
year window . Some of  the li-
quidity will be withdrawn au-
tomatically over time, he sa-
id. Higher crude prices rema-
in a risk to inflation. 
Eye on inflation

uld depend on the extent to
which higher costs are passed
through. “Crude has gone up.
July was for the Indian basket
an average of  $82 (per barrel).
Aug it has gone up to $90 (per

inly have some impact, but it
will depend again on the pass-
through,” he said.

sorbed and cushioned much
of  the oil shock, helping the

RBI will continue to watch in-
flation persistence, expecta-
tions and generalisation ahe-
ad of  the next monetary poli-
cy committee meeting on Oct
5-7. “I mean, risks are there on
both sides... The MPC will
make a reassessment of  the
growth-inflation dynamics
when it meets in a month or
so. Let me not give my assess-
ment,” he said.

TABLE TENNIS SQUAD 
FACES SELECTION ROW
Sabi.Hussain @timesofindia.com
New Delhi: A selection row has hit India’s 
table tennis campaign for the Asian 
Games, with documents accessed by TOI 
revealing how personal coach 
Soumyadeep Roy was accommodated in 
the contingent at the expense of long-
serving strength and conditioning coach 
Johnson Selvakumar Devaraj.
Roy’s name did not even figure in the 
long list of coaching and support staff 
submitted by the Table Tennis Federation 
of India (TTFI) to the Indian Olympic 
Association, while Johnson’s name was 
included in the original final-entry list.
On June 10, TTFI secretary general 
Kamlesh Mehta sent the final entry to 
the IOA, naming foreign coach Massimo 
Costantini, Sourav Chakraborty, Mamata 
Prabhu, Somnath Ghosh, Sachin Shetty, 
physio Shweta Ashok Sonone, Johnson 
as strength and conditioning coach and 
masseur Amarjit Singh.
Then on July 12, Roy wrote to TTFI 
seeking accreditation as the personal 
coach of Payas Jain, Syndrela Das and 
Sutirtha Mukherjee.
On Aug 25, TTFI recommended Roy 
but requested that no official already on 
the list be deleted. Yet, when the sports 
ministry’s sanction came on Sept 2, Roy 
was in and Johnson was out.

O
n a crisp March af -
ternoon in 1951, in -
side the newly chris-
tened National Sta -
dium in New Delhi, an infant 
republic dared to dream in 
broad daylight.
Barely four years removed 
from the trauma of  Partition 
and the dawn of  Independ -
ence, India did not merely host 
the inaugural Asian Games, 
she breathed life into it.
Under the motto ‘Ever On-
ward’, a fledgling nation gath-
ered eleven Asian delegations 
to celebrate sport, brother -
hood and human endurance.
When Sachin Nag surged 
through the water to claim 
100m freestyle gold, and Lavy 
Pinto blazed across the newly 
built cinder track to complete 
a historic sprint double (100m 
and 200m), sport became 
something far larger than po-
dium finishes. It was an affir-
mation of  identity . It was a 
statement to the world that 
India, long silenced, had ar -
rived to run, leap and fight on 
her own terms.
Yet, the decades that fol -
lowed were not an unbroken 
march of  success. For genera-
tions of  Indian sports lovers, 
the Asian Games became an 
exercise in poetic longing, a 
canvas marked by solitary 
bursts of  genius amid sys -
temic heartbreaks.
We held tight to those in -
candescent moments. We re -
membered the peerless stride 
of  ‘Flying Sikh’ Milkha Singh 
in Tokyo in 1958 where he won 
the 200m and 400m gold, the 
sheer , indomitable spirit of  PT 
Usha storming the track in 
Seoul in 1986 with four gold 
medals fluttering against her 
chest, and the bruising, pride-
drenched clashes of  our hock-
ey teams on natural turf  and 
synthetic pitch alike.
These were beacons in the 
dark, but the broader arithme-
tic remained modest. For 
years, the contingent’s tally 
hovered in the twenties, thir-
ties and forties.
Over seven decades, that 
ledger grew gradually but con-
sistently . With a lifetime tally 
of  779 medals — consisting of  
183 gold, 239 silver, and 357 
bronze — this quiet record 
captures the essence of  our 
athletic struggles and our un-
yielding drive.
Then came 2022 Hangzhou, 
an edition delayed to 2023 by 
the Covid-19 pandemic.
Something fundamental 
had shifted in the core of  In -
dian sport. The diffidence of  
old had evaporated, replaced 
by an unapologetic, ruthless 
hunger . Before the contingent 
boarded their flights, an auda-
cious phrase echoed through 
the corridors of  training cen-
tres: “Iss baar , 100 paar”.
To many , it sounded like 
wishful bravado. India had 
never achieved a 75-medal 
haul in a single edition, with 
their second-highest tally be -
ing 70 medals at Jakarta 
Games in 2018. A triple-digit 
tally was something expected 
only from traditional sporting 
powerhouses like China, Ja -
pan and South Korea, not a 
nation that had long carried 
the tag of  an underachiever .
What unfolded in Hang -
zhou was nothing short of  an 
athletic revolution. Day after 
day , the national anthem 
played with relentless regular-
ity . It was not just one disci -
pline carrying the burden; it 
was a total, across-the-board 
breakthrough.
In equestrian, four young 
riders ended a 41-year gold 
drought in dressage. At the 
shooting ranges, a new gen -
eration of  marksmen com -
peted with the composure of  
seasoned masters. On the bad-
minton courts, Satwiksairaj 
Rankireddy and Chirag Shetty 
smashed through the tradi -
tional Chinese, Malaysian and 
Korean pairs to claim a his -
toric men’s doubles crown. On 
the track, Neeraj Chopra won 
back-to-back Asian Games 
javelin gold.
When the dust settled, the 
scoreboard bore testimony to 
history: 28 gold, 38 silver and 41 
bronze. A total of  107 medals.
India had not merely 
crossed a critical threshold; 
she had smashed through it, 
securing 4th place on the 
Asian Games table.
The triple-digit barrier, 
that psychological ceiling that 
had constrained Indian ambi-
tions for over seventy years, 
lay shattered in pieces. Hang-
zhou was the turning point 
where Indian sport stopped 
knocking on the door of  glob-
al elites and simply walked in.
Now, the calendar has 
turned, and the countdown 
towards Aichi-Nagoya, Japan, 
has started.
The emotional landscape 
of  this upcoming campaign is 
entirely different. Hangzhou 
was about proving that India 
could; Aichi-Nagoya is about 
proving that Hangzhou was 
merely the foundation.
The targets are no longer 
shrouded in modesty . The man-
date for the Indian contingent 
heading to the Land of  the Ris-
ing Sun is crystal clear: break 
into the continental top three.
To stand shoulder-to-shoul-
der with  China, Japan and 
South Korea demands an es -
calation in every single arena.
More than that, Aichi-Na -
goya is going to be an essential 
stepping stone towards the 
ultimate prize, the 2028 Olym-
pic Games in Los Angeles. The 
battles fought on Japanese 
mats, tracks and courts will 
serve as the stress-test for 
Olympic destiny . The medals 
won will be the currency 
that’s going to fuel India’s LA 
2028 campaign.
